WHY THIS MATTERS

The GAP score is not just a number. Each point represents millions of households in high-risk zones with no financial buffer for the inevitable. Japan's next major earthquake is a matter of when, not if.

Source: NIED seismic hazard model (2020) · GIROJ earthquake insurance statistics FY2024 · e-Stat housing census 2020

03 · How this was built
1
We asked: how likely is an earthquake here?

Japan's government science agency (NIED) publishes a number for every prefecture: the probability of a major earthquake hitting in the next 30 years. Think of it like a weather forecast, but for the next three decades. Tokyo sits at 84%. Shizuoka at 89.7%. We collected this for all 47 prefectures.

We also collected the GIROJ risk zone — a separate official classification (Zone 1–3) that the insurance industry uses to set premiums. Zone 3 = highest risk. This gave us a second angle on the same question.

Data collected → NIED 30-year seismic probability (%) + GIROJ risk zone (1–3)
2
We asked: how many households actually have insurance?

GIROJ (the insurance industry's rating body) publishes earthquake insurance penetration rates by prefecture every year. This tells us: of all the households that could have earthquake insurance, what percentage actually do? Tokyo is at 62.2%. That sounds decent — until you pair it with an 84% earthquake probability.

We also pulled household counts from the government's 2020 housing census (e-Stat), so we could convert percentages into real numbers: how many actual families are unprotected.

Data collected → EQ insurance penetration rate (%) + household count by prefecture
3
We combined them into a single GAP score

The core idea is simple: GAP = Risk − Coverage. A prefecture with high earthquake risk but low insurance penetration scores high. A prefecture with high risk but equally high coverage scores low — meaning people are protected.

We weighted the seismic probability more heavily (70%) than the insurance zone category (30%), because the probability number is more precise and directly comparable across prefectures.

Risk score = (30yr earthquake probability × 70%) + (zone severity × 30%)

GAP score = Risk score − Insurance coverage rate

→ Positive score = more risk than coverage = underinsurance gap exists
→ Higher score = bigger gap = more urgent
4
What does the score actually mean?
+0.47
Tokyo's score. The largest gap in Japan. 84% earthquake probability, but only 62% insured. ~2.3 million unprotected households.
~0.00
A balanced prefecture. Risk and coverage are roughly matched. Not a priority for market intervention.
Negative
Coverage exceeds risk. Lower-risk prefecture with high adoption. People here are relatively well protected.

This is not a precise actuarial model — it is a strategic signal. It answers one question that matters: where should Japan's insurance industry, government, and policymakers focus their attention first?
